### Lecture 1 — The Bullwhip Effect

<audio controls src="/audio/toefl/sp05/L1.mp3"></audio>

**1.** What is the main topic of the talk?
   - (A) The role of raw material suppliers in marketing
   - (B) The history of global warehouse management
   - (C) The causes and effects of the bullwhip effect
   - (D) The benefits of increasing customer demand

**2.** The speaker states that as the original demand signal moves toward raw material suppliers, it becomes:
   - (A) More accurate due to professional verification
   - (B) A massive and artificial peak in demand
   - (C) Easier to manage via warehouse technology
   - (D) Significantly smaller due to logistical delays

**3.** What does the speaker suggest about the relationship between different sectors of the supply chain?
   - (A) They are usually located in the same geographic region
   - (B) They share too much data which leads to panic
   - (C) They prioritize consumer satisfaction over making profits
   - (D) They act independently which causes system fragility

**4.** According to the speaker, all of the following groups are involved in the over-ordering chain EXCEPT:
   - (A) Government regulators
   - (B) Retail stores
   - (C) Wholesalers
   - (D) Raw material suppliers

**Script**

Have you ever wondered why a small increase in customer demand can lead to a massive shortage of products months later? This is often caused by the 'bullwhip effect' in supply chain management. The process starts with a minor fluctuation in retail sales. When a store sees a small spike in demand, the immediate result is a slightly larger order placed with the wholesaler to ensure they don't run out. But the consequences go further than that. The wholesaler, seeing the larger order from the store, panics and places an even bigger order with the manufacturer. This sets off a chain of over-ordering at every stage of the supply chain. What many people don't realize is that by the time the message reaches the raw material suppliers, the original 'small increase' has transformed into a massive, artificial demand peak. This ripples outward to affect global shipping costs and warehouse space. Eventually, the market becomes flooded with excess inventory that no one actually wants once the initial hype dies down. This demonstrates why real-time data sharing between stores and factories is so vital for modern commerce. It makes us reflect on how fragile our global systems are when people stop talking to each other and start making guesses based on incomplete information.

**Script**

Teal organizations represent an emergent paradigm in management theory characterized by self-management, wholeness, and an evolutionary purpose. One striking feature of this model is the total absence of traditional hierarchy; instead of a pyramid of bosses, authority is distributed among self-governing teams that operate with high levels of autonomy. There is also the matter of evolutionary purpose, where the company is viewed as a living entity with its own direction, rather than a mere vehicle for hitting quarterly financial targets set by shareholders. All of these aspects come together in a system that prioritizes agility and employee engagement over rigid control structures. The net result of these factors is a workplace that can adapt to market fluctuations far more rapidly than a centralized bureaucracy. Essentially, the convergence of these three pillars creates a resilient ecosystem that thrives in the volatility of the modern global economy. Recently, this model has gained significant traction among software development firms and healthcare providers looking for sustainable alternatives to the high-burnout environments of traditional corporate structures.

We must ask ourselves: could such a decentralized, non-state entity survive in today's highly regulated global market, or was its success purely a product of that specific historical vacuum?

   - (B) A 15% decrease in brand loyalty
   - (C) Increased demand for used tech products
   - (D) Movements toward mid-tier competitors

**Script**

Last week we touched upon entry barriers, and today I want to focus on a specific pricing model called price skimming. This idea suggests that a firm can maximize profits by setting the highest possible initial price for a new product, then gradually lowering it over time. For decades, the accepted view was that this strategy worked best for high-tech goods with no immediate competition. The standard reasoning was that early adopters are willing to pay a premium for the 'latest and greatest,' while the subsequent price drops attract more budget-conscious segments. This behavior was widely linked to the perceived prestige of being first. But recent evidence challenges this view. A comprehensive analysis of smartphone sales data from the last five years revealed that aggressive skimming can actually damage a brand's reputation and push customers toward mid-tier competitors who offer stable pricing. In several major markets, researchers discovered that consistently high initial costs led to a 15% decrease in long-term brand loyalty. This indicates that modern consumers value price transparency and fairness more than the novelty of a new release. What this points to is a need for companies to rethink the 'harvesting' phase of their product lifecycle if they want to keep their customers for years rather than just months. This discovery has significant implications for how startups should value their initial offerings in a crowded digital marketplace.

Instead of talking more, people retreated into their headphones and used instant messaging to avoid the noise and lack of privacy. What this points to is that physical proximity doesn't guarantee psychological openness. The irony is that the very design intended to foster community ended up creating a 'silent office' where digital communication replaced real human speech.
