### Lecture 1 — Biological Buffer Systems

<audio controls src="/audio/toefl/sp3/L1.mp3"></audio>

1. What is the main topic of the talk?
   - (A) How chemical buffer systems maintain stability
   - (B) The process of manufacturing bicarbonate
   - (C) The creation of mechanical shock absorbers
   - (D) The history of marine life in the ocean

2. Which of the following best summarizes the main point of the lecture?
   - (A) The bicarbonate system is more effective than mechanical car springs.
   - (B) Mechanical devices and biological systems react identically to pressure.
   - (C) Buffer systems are indestructible chemical mechanisms found in nature.
   - (D) Biological buffers are essential but have specific functional limits.

3. According to the speaker, what is a biological buffer system?
   - (A) A method for increasing lactic acid
   - (B) A type of physical car spring
   - (C) A mechanism that maintains stable pH
   - (D) A way to grow stronger coral reefs

4. According to the speaker, what happens to the bicarbonate buffer system during intense exercise?
   - (A) It converts blood into a thick physical shock absorber.
   - (B) It increases the production of lactic acid in the muscles.
   - (C) It signals the carpal muscles to stop working immediately.
   - (D) It prevents the blood from becoming excessively acidic.

**Script**

In chemistry, a shock absorber refers to a mechanical device that dampens the impact of a physical blow to prevent damage. Think of it this way: just as a car needs springs to handle a bumpy road, our bodies need a chemical version to handle sudden changes. The parallel here is a biological buffer system, which keeps our internal pH stable even when we eat or drink something acidic. In the human body, the most important one is the bicarbonate buffer system. Take the example of intense exercise: when your muscles produce lactic acid, it enters the bloodstream. Instead of your blood becoming too acidic, the buffer system reacts quickly to neutralize it. Now, think about the ocean's coral reefs as a second example. The seawater acts as a massive buffer that absorbs carbon dioxide from the air. This keeps the ocean's acidity at a level where marine life can still build healthy shells. However, there is a limitation to these systems. Just like a mechanical shock absorber can break if the impact is too strong, biological buffers have a capacity limit. If the environment changes too fast or too much, the buffer can't keep up, which is what we see happening with ocean acidification today. Understanding these limits is key to seeing how sensitive life really is to chemical changes.

   - (A) It creates a legal framework for the status of cloned animals.
   - (B) It allows scientists to insert ancient genetic material into living organisms.
   - (C) It helps scientists identify which species are likely to go extinct next.
   - (D) It is used to preserve the permafrost layers in the Siberian tundra.

4. What specifically does the professor mention about the role of mammoths in the tundra?
   - (A) They would serve as a primary food source for modern predators.
   - (B) Their migration patterns would create new paths for human researchers.
   - (C) Their presence would lead to the destruction of excess grass growth.
   - (D) Their movement would help prevent the permafrost from thawing.

**Script**

Today, we're going to talk about the controversial concept of de-extinction. De-extinction refers to the process of resurrecting an extinct species through advanced genomic tools and cloning techniques, such as using the DNA of a woolly mammoth to modify the cells of a modern elephant. This has gained traction because of recent breakthroughs in CRISPR gene-editing technology, which allow scientists to precisely insert ancient genetic sequences into living hosts. The benefit is the potential restoration of lost ecological functions; for instance, reintroducing mammoths to the Siberian tundra could theoretically help maintain the permafrost and mitigate climate change by trampling snow and promoting grass growth. This could invigorate damaged ecosystems and restore natural balances that were destroyed by human activity or ancient climatic shifts. On the other hand, significant ethical and practical challenges persist. Critics argue that the environments these animals once inhabited have changed so drastically that the resurrected species might not survive, or worse, could become invasive pests that threaten existing biodiversity. Furthermore, allocating massive financial resources to bringing back the 'dead' might divert crucial funding from the conservation of currently endangered species. Next, we will discuss the legal status of such organisms and after that: the specific methodological hurdles involved in extracting viable DNA from specimens preserved in late-Pleistocene permafrost layers.

   - (B) To compare the efficiency of biological light with modern technology

   - (D) To explain the chemical basis of a biological trait and its various roles

3. According to the speaker, what is the role of luciferase in the chemical reaction?
   - (A) It regulates the temperature of the cold light.
   - (B) It serves as a catalyst to accelerate the reaction.
   - (C) It acts as a fuel source that is consumed during the glow.
   - (D) It provides the raw material that burns to create light.

4. According to the lecture, what defines the light produced by these organisms as "cold light"?
   - (A) The light requires freezing oxygen to start the chemical reaction.
   - (B) The light is produced by organisms living in freezing water.
   - (C) The light is only visible in extremely low temperatures.
   - (D) The light production involves very little thermal energy loss.

**Script**

Today's topic is the incredible ability of certain deep-sea creatures to create their own light, a phenomenon scientists call bioluminescence. In the pitch-black environment of the midnight zone, being able to glow is a major survival advantage. The mechanism works as follows. It involves a chemical reaction inside the organism's body. This process begins when a molecule called luciferin reacts with oxygen. What enables this is a specific enzyme known as luciferase, which acts as a catalyst to speed up the reaction without getting used up itself. The sequence involves the release of energy in the form of cold light, meaning almost no heat is produced during the glow. This is crucial because it prevents the animal from wasting energy or damaging its own tissues in the high-pressure depths of the ocean. Many fish use this light for communication, to attract a mate, or to lure unsuspecting prey into their mouths. Some even use it as a form of camouflage to hide their shadows from predators swimming below them. However, ocean acidification is a growing threat because it can change the chemical balance of seawater. This might interfere with the pH levels needed for these delicate enzymes to work properly. The significance of understanding bioluminescence goes beyond biology, as researchers are now using these same light-producing proteins in medical imaging to track how diseases spread through human cells.

   - (A) By producing sugary fruits for birds
   - (B) By utilizing air currents to transport pollen
   - (C) By releasing seeds into the water
   - (D) Through the assistance of nectar-seeking insects

4. The speaker states that angiosperms are currently significant to humanity because:
   - (A) They serve as the main source of modern antibiotics
   - (B) They provide the majority of human food energy
   - (C) They stabilize the global climate through scent
   - (D) They are the only group that survives in all climates

**Script**

You're probably familiar with the vibrant colors and scents of modern flowers, but the world was not always so aesthetically diverse. Its origins trace back to the early Cretaceous period, approximately one hundred and forty million years ago. Before this era, the terrestrial landscape was dominated by gymnosperms, such as conifers and cycads, which relied primarily on the wind for reproduction. A defining characteristic of the emerging angiosperms was the development of the flower itself, a specialized reproductive structure designed to attract animal pollinators. The turning point came when these plants began to co-evolve with insects, creating a symbiotic relationship that accelerated the diversification of both groups. By the mid-Cretaceous, it had become clear that flowering plants possessed a distinct competitive advantage, as their protected seeds within fruits allowed for superior dispersal by animals. This evolutionary leap triggered what Charles Darwin famously described as an 'abominable mystery' due to the startlingly rapid appearance of diverse species in the fossil record. Today, it continues to be the dominant plant group on Earth, comprising over ninety percent of all terrestrial plant species and providing the primary caloric basis for human civilization. This vast biological success illustrates how a single structural innovation can transform the entire ecological architecture of the planet. What began as a local botanical shift has now become the fundamental backbone of nearly every terrestrial ecosystem on the globe.

3. According to the speaker, what is parthenogenesis?
   - (A) Development of an embryo from an unfertilized egg
   - (B) A ritual used to attract mates in the wild
   - (C) A method used by scientists to clone animals
   - (D) The process of genetic mixing between species

4. The speaker states that a primary advantage of parthenogenesis for a species is:
   - (A) Increased resistance to environmental viruses
   - (B) The ability to produce stronger male offspring
   - (C) The creation of more diverse offspring
   - (D) Preservation of energy during the reproductive cycle

**Script**

Today, we're going to talk about a unique form of reproduction known as parthenogenesis. Parthenogenesis refers to a process where an embryo develops from an unfertilized egg, meaning offspring are produced without a male genetic contribution. In many ways, it is like a form of natural cloning. This has gained traction because it allows certain species to survive in environments where mates are hard to find. The benefit is clear for isolated populations; for example, a female Komodo dragon in a zoo or on a distant island can still produce young even if no males are around. This ensures that the population doesn't go extinct in the short term. Furthermore, it saves the energy that would usually be spent on finding a partner and performing mating rituals. On the other hand, there are major long-term risks. Because the offspring are genetic copies or very similar to the mother, there is a total lack of genetic diversity. This makes the whole population more vulnerable to diseases or changes in the climate because they all have the same weaknesses. If a virus can kill one individual, it can likely kill them all. This balance between immediate survival and long-term adaptation is a central theme in evolutionary biology. It shows how nature prioritizes the continuation of a species, even if it comes at the cost of future flexibility. The question remains: how will future generations balance these genetic disadvantages if the environment changes suddenly?

   - (A) The heart rate becomes faster and more shallow
   - (B) The body temperature rises to fight infection
   - (C) The metabolic rate decreases significantly
   - (D) The animal's brain activity increases

4. What does the speaker state about the frequency of torpor in hummingbirds?
   - (A) They only enter the state during the winter months
   - (B) They use it as a response to sudden predator attacks
   - (C) They utilize it on a nightly basis to survive
   - (D) They enter it once a week to conserve digestive energy

**Script**

Torpor is a biological practice that many small animals use to survive harsh conditions. It is a state of decreased physiological activity, usually by a reduced body temperature and metabolic rate. This serves multiple functions, including saving energy during cold nights and helping animals survive when food is very scarce. Small birds like hummingbirds often go into torpor every single night to prevent starving before morning. What's often misunderstood is the difference between torpor and regular sleep. For those unfamiliar with animal physiology, it may appear that the animal is just having a deep nap. However, during torpor, the heart rate can drop from hundreds of beats per minute to just a few, and the animal cannot wake up instantly if a predator approaches. This perception led to early naturalists thinking these animals were actually dead or diseased when they were found in this state. Because they look so stiff and cold, people sometimes tried to 'rescue' them, which actually harmed the animals. Today, the current status of research shows that torpor is much more common than we once thought. Scientists are even studying how this state works to see if humans could ever use a similar process for long-distance space travel. It is a fascinating survival strategy that highlights how life adapts to extreme energy shortages in the natural world.

3. According to the speaker, how does the black spruce release its seeds?
   - (A) By releasing them when animals eat the resin
   - (B) By waiting for the rainy season to soften the cones
   - (C) By using intense heat to melt the resin on its cones
   - (D) By spreading them through underground root systems

4. According to the professor, what is the function of epicormic buds in eucalyptus trees?
   - (A) They protect the seeds from being consumed by local animals.
   - (B) They produce volatile oils that help the tree catch fire.
   - (C) They prevent the thick bark from cracking during a fire.
   - (D) They enable the tree to grow new shoots from the trunk after a fire.

**Script**

Our lecture today examines the diverse ecological strategies that various plant communities have evolved to survive and even thrive within fire-prone environments worldwide. In the Boreal forests of Canada, we find species such as the black spruce, which utilizes serotinous cones that remain tightly sealed by resin for years. These cones only open to release their seeds when the intense heat of a wildfire melts the resin, ensuring the next generation germinates in nutrient-rich, post-fire ash. Meanwhile, in the Australian outback, eucalyptus trees exhibit a remarkably different adaptation through the presence of epicormic buds located deep beneath their thick, insulating bark. These dormant buds allow the tree to rapidly sprout new green shoots directly from its trunk almost immediately after the flames have passed. A striking contrast emerges in the Mediterranean basin, where certain shrubs possess volatile oils that actually encourage fire, effectively clearing out competitors while their own heat-resistant seeds wait in the soil. In contrast, the fire-resistant savannas of Africa feature grasses that store the majority of their biomass underground, shielded from thermal damage. Despite these differences, a common thread is the reliance on periodic disturbance to maintain biodiversity. Across all these traditions of survival, we observe that fire is not merely a destructive force but a fundamental ecological architect. Such niche specializations illustrate how distinct biological lineages converge upon the necessity of adaptation to survive extreme environmental fluctuations.

### Lecture 8 — Keystone Species

<audio controls src="/audio/toefl/sp3/L8.mp3"></audio>

1. What is the lecture mainly about?
   - (A) The classification of different forest types
   - (B) The biological structure of cell membranes
   - (C) The role of keystone species in ecosystems
   - (D) The history of gray wolves in Yellowstone

2. What aspect of biological essentialism does the speaker focus on?
   - (A) The failure of reintroduction programs in national parks
   - (B) The evolution of the term selectivity in modern biology
   - (C) The need to prioritize cellular research over conservation
   - (D) The comparison between cellular mechanics and ecological regulation

3. According to the speaker, what is 'selectivity' in the context of biology?
   - (A) The method used to count bird species
   - (B) The process of sea urchins eating kelp
   - (C) The ability of a membrane to filter substances
   - (D) The way wolves choose which elk to hunt

4. What does the speaker state about the effect of sea otters on their environment?
   - (A) They compete with birds for habitat in kelp forests.
   - (B) They destroy kelp forests to find sea urchins.
   - (C) They cause ecosystems to collapse into urchin barrens.
   - (D) They maintain the habitat for hundreds of other organisms.

**Script**

In cellular biology, the term 'selectivity' refers to the ability of a membrane to allow only certain substances through while blocking others. Think of it this way: a cell membrane acts like a high-security gatekeeper that maintains the internal environment of the cell regardless of the chaos outside. The parallel here is the concept of a 'keystone species' in conservation ecology. Just as a gatekeeper controls the flow of a building, a keystone species regulates the entire structure of an ecosystem. Take the classic example of gray wolves in Yellowstone National Park. When they were reintroduced, they didn't just hunt elk; they changed the behavior of the entire ecosystem, leading to the regrowth of trees and the return of various bird species. Now, think about the sea otter in the kelp forests of the Pacific. By eating sea urchins, otters prevent the urchins from destroying the kelp, which provides a habitat for hundreds of other organisms. Without the otter, the entire system collapses into an 'urchin barren.' These examples show that the health of an environment often depends on just one or two critical players. However, a potential downside of focusing exclusively on keystone species is that we might neglect the broader biodiversity of an area. Modern conservationists worry that by pouring all resources into one 'celebrity' species, we might ignore smaller, less visible organisms that perform vital functions like soil aeration or pollination. This reminds us that even small changes can have profound effects on the stability of our natural world.
