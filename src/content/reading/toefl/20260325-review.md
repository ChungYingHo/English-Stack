### Colonial House-Building

Seventeenth-century colonial houses were simple, functional structures reflecting medieval traditions. In the first half of the eighteenth century a shift toward greater elegance occurred as rising prosperity enabled colonists to build finer dwellings. Because architecture was not yet a profession in the colonies, designs were often produced by informed amateurs or by carpenters consulting English architectural manuals; surviving library inventories show many such handbooks, and their influence appears in period houses. Yet builders adapted rules freely, producing a wide divergence of tastes.

Regional materials shaped construction. New England generally favored wood, though brick became common in Boston and other towns where the danger of fire encouraged more durable material. Stone was used widely only in Pennsylvania and adjacent areas. Virginia and Maryland saw increased brick use in houses and outbuildings, but many wealthy owners still built in wood. In the Carolinas, even crowded Charleston, wooden houses predominated over brick.

Interiors improved markedly: windows grew larger and shutters were removed as small leaded panes gave way to larger clear glass; doorways became more decorative and fireplaces assumed ornamented roles. Walls were often plastered or elaborately paneled in wood. Color schemes shifted toward white paint instead of earlier blues, yellows, and greens, and after about 1730 colonial newspapers began advertising scenic patterned wallpapers — evidence of growing taste for refined interior decoration.
